FICTIONAL INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT (synthetic teaching material, not a real person)
Study: Belonging, migration and neighbourhood life in Indian cities
Interview 03 | Pseudonym: Sunita Devi | 39 | Woman | Delhi, peri-urban (unauthorised colony) | Migrant from Uttar Pradesh
Interview date: 26 January 2025 | Language: Hindi, translated by the research team
Hindi words are kept where the speaker used them, with a gloss in square brackets.
Place names in this transcript are invented.

I: Namaste, Sunita-ji. Thank you for letting me come to your home. Is it all right if I record our conversation?

R: Haan, record karo [yes, record it]. But don't put my real name, okay? My husband doesn't know I am talking to you. [laughs] No, no, he knows, but he said don't say anything about politics.

I: Of course. We will use a different name and nothing that identifies you. Could you start by telling me how you came to live in this colony?

R: I am from Bahraich district, in UP. I came to Delhi after marriage, in 2004. I was nineteen. My husband was already working here, in a factory in the industrial area, making plastic chairs. First we lived in a jhuggi [shack in an informal settlement] near the railway line. Then in 2010 that jhuggi cluster was demolished. The bulldozer came in the morning, we had one hour to take our things. [pause] We lost many things. My children's school books, my wedding saree. After that we came here, to Shanti Kunj colony. My husband's cousin had bought a small plot here, he gave us one room on rent, and later we bought our own small plot. Fifty gaj [about 42 square metres].

I: What was the colony like when you first came?

R: Nothing was here. Only fields, and some houses, kaccha [not permanent, mud and tin]. No road, no electricity. We took electricity by hooking wire from the pole. [laughs] Everyone did that. Water from a hand pump. Slowly, slowly people built. Now see, all pucca [brick and cement] houses, two-storey, three-storey. But it is still "unauthorised." That word, sir... madam, sorry. [laughs] That word follows us.

I: What does "unauthorised" mean for your everyday life?

R: It means everything is half. We have electricity now, meters came in 2015. But water is not there. No pipeline. We buy from tanker, or there is a government tanker that comes, but when it comes, there is a fight. Every day, a fight. Women with buckets and drums, pushing, shouting. Last summer one woman's head was cut, somebody's bucket hit her. The private tanker is eight hundred rupees for one thousand litres. Every week we need it. Where does that money come from? From my work only.

I: What work do you do?

R: I work in houses, in the big society across the main road, in Sector Nine. Jhaadu-pocha, bartan [sweeping and mopping, washing dishes]. Four houses. Morning seven to one. Then I come home, cook, the children come from school. In the evening I do stitching work at home, blouses, salwar suits, for the ladies in the colony. Total I earn maybe twelve, thirteen thousand.

I: How do you travel to work?

R: Walking. It is two kilometres. There is a shortcut through the empty ground, twenty minutes, but I don't take it. Not after one incident. [pause] Three years back some men were sitting there drinking, early morning, and one of them followed me. Saying dirty things. I ran. After that I go by the main road, it takes forty minutes, but there are people. In winter, in December, January, when it is dark at seven in the morning, I am scared. The streetlights on our road, half don't work. I have told my employer madam that I cannot come before seven in winter. She was not happy, but what can I do?

I: That sounds very stressful. Are other women in the colony facing the same thing?

R: All of us, madam. All of us. Every woman here will tell you the same. The bus stop is near the drain bridge, it is very dark there. The girls going to college, they go in groups of four, five. Nobody goes alone. My daughter is in class eleven, her school is in the next colony, she goes with three other girls, and my son goes to drop them. Every day. He grumbles, but I tell him, "your sister's safety is your duty." [laughs] My husband says girls should not study so far. But I said, I did not study, I will make sure she studies.

I: Can you tell me about that conversation with your husband?

R: [pause] He is not a bad man. But he thinks like the village. When my daughter wanted to join computer class in the evening, in the market, he said no. "What will people say? Girl coming back at eight at night." We fought for one week. Then I went with her myself for the first month, sitting outside the class. [laughs] After that he agreed, because Meena's daughter from the next lane also joined, so they come together. Now my daughter wants to go to college, Delhi University. She says she will take the metro. The metro station is now only four kilometres away, it opened last year. For her generation, the metro is freedom. For me, even the bus was a big thing.

I: That is a big change across generations. Do you feel the metro changed things for women here?

R: Yes, a lot. The metro has lights, it has guards, there is a ladies' coach. The girls feel safe inside. The problem is getting to the metro. From here to the station, the e-rickshaw goes, but after eight, nine o'clock there are few, and the road is dark. So the metro is safe, but the last part, from the station to home, is not safe. That is always the problem. The last part.

I: You mentioned Meena, your neighbour. Tell me about the people you know here.

R: Oh, Meena is my sister now. [laughs] Not real sister, but more than that. She is from Bihar, I am from UP, but we are the same. We are in the same self-help group, the samooh [group]. Twelve women. Every month we each put two hundred rupees. If somebody needs money, for a hospital, for a wedding, for school fees, she can borrow from the group, very small interest. Otherwise the moneylender takes ten percent per month! [pause] When my husband had typhoid and could not work for two months, the samooh gave me fifteen thousand. Without that, I don't know.

I: How did the self-help group start?

R: An NGO madam came in 2017, she called the women for a meeting in the temple courtyard. First only four women came. The men said, "What is this, these women will go around making trouble." [laughs] But slowly more joined. Now we meet every second Sunday. We talk about money, but also everything else. Whose husband is drinking, whose daughter is getting married, which ration shop is cheating. That meeting is the only time I sit down in the whole month. [laughs] Only time I talk freely.

I: What have the women in the group been able to do together?

R: Many things. Once the ration dealer was giving less wheat, and marking full in the register. We all went together, twelve women, and stood in front of his shop. He got scared and gave correctly. [laughs] Also we went to the MLA's office about the water. Two times. He gave a letter, he said pipeline will come before the election. Election came, election went. [laughs] Pipeline didn't come. But at least now the government tanker comes more regularly, three days a week, because we complained.

I: Tell me about the local politics. Who do people go to when they have a problem?

R: There is the pradhan [local leader, here the head of the residents' association], Sharma-ji. He has been here from the beginning, he has the biggest house. Everything goes through him. If you want the tanker to come to your lane, if you want a letter for your ration card, you go to Sharma-ji. And he is close with the party. Before elections the party people come, they give blankets, they promise regularisation. "Your colony will be regular, you will get registry papers for your house." Every election the same promise. Twenty years.

I: What would regularisation mean to you?

R: [long pause] It would mean I can sleep. Honestly. Every time we hear there is a demolition drive somewhere, in some other colony, I cannot sleep for many nights. I think, this house, which we built with fifteen years of money, one brick at a time, what if they come? My husband took loan from his employer to build the second floor. If the bulldozer comes, we have nothing, and still the loan. After what happened in 2010, you can never feel fully safe. People say "this colony is too big now, they will not break it, too many votes." Maybe. But I don't trust. The paper is what matters. Without the paper, the house is not really yours.

I: Do you feel that you belong here, in this colony?

R: [pause] Yes and no. My children were born here. For them this is home. My son doesn't even like going to the village, he says there is nothing there, no internet. [laughs] My daughter, when relatives ask where she is from, she says "Dilli." So for them, yes. For me... I have lived in Delhi twenty years, longer than I lived in my village. But in my heart the village is still there. The mango trees, my mother's courtyard. When I die, I want... [pause] Anyway. But when I go to the village now, they call me "Dilli-wali." And here in Delhi, the society people call us "colony-wale," "bahar ke log" [outside people]. So where do I belong? In this lane, with Meena, with my samooh. That is my place. Not Delhi, not the village. This lane.

I: That is very meaningful. Do you feel the people in the society where you work see you as part of the city?

R: No, madam. [laughs] For them we are the maids. In some houses they give us tea in a separate cup. In one house the madam does not let me use the lift, I have to take the stairs to the eighth floor. There is a separate "service lift," but it is always broken. One day I was very tired and I took the main lift, and a resident complained to the society office. The guard told me, "next time you will not be allowed inside." [pause] These are educated people. But inside, they think we are different. Dirty.

I: How do you cope with that?

R: What can I do? I need the work. I keep my face straight, I do my work, I come home. In the samooh we talk about it, and we laugh about the madams. [laughs] That helps. And one madam, Kapoor madam, she is very good. She paid my daughter's school fees when we were short, she gives me her old clothes but nice ones, not torn. She asks about my children. So not everybody is the same.

I: What about the environment in the colony, the air, the garbage?

R: Oh, the air in winter is very bad. Now also, you can see, the sky is grey. My son has breathing problems, the doctor said it is from pollution. In November the schools closed for ten days. Also, near the colony there is a garbage dump, the landfill, and when they burn it, the smoke comes into the houses. And the drain behind our lane is open, black water, mosquitoes. In the monsoon it overflows and the dirty water comes into the lane. Children play in it. What can you do? There is no other place for them to play. There is no park in the whole colony.

I: If you could change one thing in the colony, what would it be?

R: Water. [laughs] Water first, then everything else. If there was water in the pipe, the women would get two hours back every day. Two hours! Think what we could do with two hours. My daughter would not have to miss school to stand in the tanker line. And second, lights. Streetlights on the road to the bus stop and the metro. If there is light, the girls can go out. It is that simple. The government thinks about big things, flyovers, metros. But for a woman here, a working streetlight is the difference between going to college and staying at home.

I: That is a very strong point. Is there anything else you want to say?

R: Only that we are not "unauthorised" people. The colony is unauthorised, maybe, but we are not. We work in their houses, we make their chairs, we drive their cars. We pay for everything, even for water, which in their societies comes free in the tap. [pause] Write that. And write that the women here are strong. We are managing everything. Nobody sees it, but we are managing.

I: Thank you so much, Sunita-ji. I will make sure to write that.

R: Now eat something before you go. I made poha. No, no, you cannot say no. [laughs]

[End of recording]
