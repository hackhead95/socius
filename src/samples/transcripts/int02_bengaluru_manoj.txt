FICTIONAL INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT (synthetic teaching material, not a real person)
Study: Belonging, migration and neighbourhood life in Indian cities
Interview 02 | Pseudonym: Manoj Paswan | 26 | Man | Bengaluru, peri-urban | Migrant from Bihar
Interview date: 2 March 2025 | Language: Hindi and English, translated by the research team
Hindi and Kannada words are kept where the speaker used them, with a gloss in square brackets.
Place names in this transcript are invented.

I: Thanks for meeting me, Manoj. I know you just came off a shift. Is this a good time?

R: Haan, it's okay, sir. Today I did only morning, six to two. Evening I will log in again maybe, after seven, because evening orders are more, incentive is more. [laughs] So we have one hour, no problem.

I: Great. Could you start by telling me how you came to Bengaluru?

R: I am from Samastipur district, in Bihar. Village, you know, farming family. We have two bigha land, which is nothing, my father and uncle share it. I did my BA from a college in the district, but BA from there... [laughs] it has no value, sir. I tried for railway exams, SSC, two years. Didn't clear. Then my cousin, Pintu-bhaiya, he was already here in Bengaluru, working in a warehouse. He said come, there is work, at least you will earn. So in 2021, after the second lockdown, I came. Train, forty-two hours. [laughs]

I: What was it like when you first arrived?

R: Very difficult, sir, honestly. First thing, I did not know anything. The language, I did not know even one word of Kannada. The food, everything is rice, sambar, I was missing roti so much. [laughs] And the weather was good, that I liked. But I stayed with Pintu-bhaiya and four other boys in one room. One room, sir, five people. Near the warehouse area, Nagadevi Layout. Rent was six thousand, divided by five. We slept on the floor, in rows. [laughs] Like a train compartment.

I: How did you find work?

R: Through Pintu-bhaiya only. First six months I was in the warehouse, loading, packing. Twelve hours, fourteen thousand per month. Then one friend said delivery is better, if you have a bike you can earn twenty-five, thirty thousand. So I took a second-hand bike on loan. Now I do food delivery and sometimes the quick-commerce, grocery in ten minutes, that one. [laughs] Ten minutes, sir! They put that in the ad, but it is us who are running on the road.

I: Tell me more about that. What is a typical day like?

R: I wake up at five, five-thirty. Log in by six. Morning is breakfast orders, office people. Afternoon slow. Evening seven to eleven is the peak. I do maybe twenty-five, thirty orders on a good day. The main thing is the roads, sir. Potholes everywhere, and in the rain, the roads flood, underpass full of water. Last October I fell, the bike slipped in the water near the flyover. My hand... [shows wrist] see, still it pains. Two weeks I could not work. No work, no money. The company gave some insurance form, but it was so complicated, I gave up.

I: Who helped you during those two weeks?

R: The boys only. My roommates. They paid my share of rent, one of them cooked for me. And there is a WhatsApp group, "Samastipur Boys Bengaluru" [laughs], around two hundred people, all from our district. Somebody posted about my accident and three, four people sent money on UPI. Five hundred, one thousand. Nobody asked for it back. That group is very important. If somebody needs a room, a job, if somebody's father is sick back home and he needs to borrow for a ticket, everything goes in the group.

I: So that network is like a support system.

R: Yes, sir, it is our family here. Without that I would have gone back in the first month. When somebody new comes from the village, we go to the station to receive him, we give him a place to sleep, we teach him how to use the app, which roads have police checking. [laughs]

I: You mentioned the police. Can you say more?

R: [pause] See, sir, it is not always bad. But sometimes, if they see a delivery boy, and they hear you speaking Hindi, they will stop you. Papers check, helmet check, then "chalan" [fine] or they say pay two hundred and go. With a Kannada person they will not do that so much. Once a policeman said, "You people come from the north and make all the problems here." I didn't say anything. What can I say? I just want to go home safely.

I: How did that make you feel?

R: Bura lagta hai [it feels bad]. We are working, we are not doing anything wrong. We deliver their food, their groceries, at midnight also, in the rain also. But we are still "outside people." [pause] Also with the landlords. That is the biggest problem.

I: Tell me about housing.

R: Sir, finding a room is the hardest thing in this city. First, many owners will not give to bachelors. "Family only." Second, some will ask your name and when they hear "Paswan," or they hear you are from Bihar, they say "already rented." [laughs bitterly] The same room is still on the board the next day. Third, the deposit. They ask ten months' deposit! For a single room, rent is eight thousand, deposit eighty thousand. Where will I get eighty thousand?

I: Where do you live now?

R: Now I live in a PG [paying guest accommodation], shared room, three people, in Hosakere Extension. It is further out, near the new apartments. Rent is six thousand five hundred for my bed, with food. Food is not good, but okay. [laughs] Before this I was in a room with two friends, but the owner increased the rent from nine to twelve thousand in one year. He said "the metro is coming, the area is developing." We could not pay, so he gave one month notice and we had to leave. That was my third move in three years.

I: What is it like, moving so often?

R: It means you don't settle, sir. You don't buy anything big, not a cupboard, not a TV, because you know you will move again. All my things fit in one bag and one box. [laughs] And you don't know your neighbours. Why would you? In six months you will be somewhere else. The people in the apartments around, they don't talk to us. They see us as delivery boys, security guards, drivers. Service people. Even in the lift, some apartments have a separate lift for delivery people. Separate lift, sir! I have delivered to buildings where they make you leave the food at the gate and the customer does not even look at you.

I: How do you feel about the neighbourhood you live in now?

R: Neighbourhood... [pause] I don't know if I have a neighbourhood. There is my PG, there is the chai stall at the corner where all the delivery boys wait between orders, there is the mobile repair shop, and a small Bihari dhaba [roadside eatery] where we get litti-chokha on Sundays. That is my area. The chai stall owner, he is from Tamil Nadu, Murugan-anna. He speaks a little Hindi now because of us. [laughs] He lets us charge our phones there, he keeps our helmets sometimes. That chai stall is like my adda, my meeting place.

I: Have you learned any Kannada?

R: Swalpa swalpa [a little, a little]. [laughs] "Gothilla" [I don't know], "banni" [come], "yeshtu" [how much], "saaku" [enough]. For delivery you learn the basic. Left, right, which cross, which main. And I found that if I say even two words in Kannada, people are nicer. The security guards smile. One aunty, she gave me a banana and said "good boy, you are learning." [laughs] So I am trying. But my friends make fun of me, "Kya re, you have become Kannadiga?"

I: Do you feel you belong in Bengaluru?

R: [long pause] Honestly, sir? No. Not yet. Bengaluru is for work. Home is my village. When I say "ghar jaana hai" [I want to go home], I mean Samastipur. My mother is there, my younger sister. I send twelve thousand every month for her studies. I want her to do nursing. But when I go to the village for Chhath Puja, after one week I get bored. [laughs] There is no internet properly, nothing to do, and the village people also say "you have become a city boy." So I am... in between. Not here, not there.

I: That is a very interesting feeling, being in between. Can you say more?

R: Like, here I am a Bihari, an outsider. There I am the one who went to Bengaluru. Neither place is fully mine. But slowly, I think, maybe Bengaluru is becoming a little bit mine. When I go to some areas now, I know every road, every shortcut, which apartment has which guard. I know the city better than many people who were born here, sir! [laughs] I know where the good biryani is at two in the night. That is also a kind of belonging, no?

I: Absolutely. What about safety? You work late at night.

R: For us boys, the main danger is the road. Accidents. Cars, buses, they don't see us. And the pressure of time, the app shows the customer "arriving in 8 minutes," so we have to rush. Also some areas at night, there are drunk people, sometimes they fight. One time some boys tried to take my phone near the lake road at one in the morning. I just drove away fast. But for girls it is worse. There are some girls doing delivery now, very few. They don't take night shifts. One girl in our hub, she said a customer opened the door and asked her to come inside. She stopped doing evening after that.

I: What about municipal services where you live? Water, garbage?

R: Water is from tanker. The PG owner buys tanker water, and in summer there is shortage, so there are rules, bath only in morning, one bucket. [laughs] The borewell has gone dry. Garbage, they come to the main road, but in the inner roads people throw on the empty plots. And the roads, I told you, full of potholes. The BBMP [Bengaluru municipal corporation] people, I have never seen them. Our PG owner deals with all that. We don't vote here, our vote is in Bihar, so nobody comes to us for anything. For the politicians here, we do not exist.

I: That is an important point. You are not registered to vote here.

R: No, sir. And honestly, even in Bihar, last time I could not go for voting because they don't give leave. No work, no pay. So I don't vote anywhere. [laughs] Who will listen to us?

I: Looking ahead, where do you see yourself in five years?

R: I want to save and start something. Maybe a small shop, mobile accessories, or a cloud kitchen, Bihari food. Litti-chokha, sattu paratha. Bengaluru people like to try new food. [laughs] If it works, maybe I will bring my sister here after nursing, there are many hospitals. And maybe get married. My mother is already asking. [laughs] If I get married, I will need a proper room, not a PG. A family room. Then maybe the owners will give, because "family only." [laughs] That is my plan. But sir, plans are plans. One accident and everything is finished.

I: What would make you feel more at home here?

R: Respect, sir. Just normal respect. We are not asking for anything special. If the apartment people say "thank you," if the landlord gives a room without asking caste and state, if the police don't stop us just for the language. And a proper place to rest. There are thousands of delivery boys in this city and nowhere to sit, no toilet, no water. We sit on the footpath outside the restaurants. If there was a small rest point, with shade and water and toilet, that would make a big difference. Small things.

I: Is there anything else you want to share?

R: Only this, sir. When you write your report, write that we also are building this city. The construction workers, the delivery boys, the guards, the maids. All from outside. Without us, Bengaluru will stop in one day. But nobody thinks of us as Bengaluru people. [pause] Maybe after ten years. Maybe my children will be Bengaluru people. [laughs] They will speak Kannada better than me.

I: Thank you, Manoj. This was really helpful.

R: Thank you, sir. Okay, I have to log in now, it is almost seven. [laughs]

[End of recording]
