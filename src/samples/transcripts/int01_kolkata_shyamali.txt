FICTIONAL INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT (synthetic teaching material, not a real person)
Study: Belonging, migration and neighbourhood life in Indian cities
Interview 01 | Pseudonym: Shyamali Basu | 58 | Woman | Kolkata, north, core city | Born in Kolkata
Interview date: 8 February 2025 | Language: Bengali and English, translated by the research team
Bengali words are kept where the speaker used them, with a gloss in square brackets.

I: Thank you for making time, Shyamali-di. To begin, could you tell me how long you have lived in this neighbourhood?

R: Oh, all my life, you can say. I was born in this house, actually in the room upstairs, my mother did not go to hospital for me. [laughs] So fifty-eight years. I left for two years after marriage, we lived in Behala with my in-laws, and then my husband got a transfer and we came back here because my father was alone. So this para [neighbourhood] is... it is not a place I live, it is who I am. I don't know how else to say it.

I: What do you mean when you say it is who you are?

R: See, when I walk from the bus stop to my house, it is maybe seven minutes. But it takes me twenty-five. [laughs] Because the man at the medicine shop will ask about my knee, then Mitra-babu's wife will be on her balcony, then the boys at the club will say "Mashima [aunty], your light bill came?" because they help me pay online. Everybody knows my father's name, my grandfather's name. When my husband died, four years back, I did not have to arrange anything. Not one thing. The club boys went to the crematorium, the neighbours cooked for eleven days. That is what I mean. You cannot buy that.

I: That sounds like a very close community. Has it always been like that?

R: [pause] More then than now. When I was a girl, every house had a rowak, you know, the raised platform in front, where the old men sat in the evening for adda [long informal conversation]. Politics, football, East Bengal-Mohun Bagan, somebody's daughter's marriage, everything. We girls were not allowed to sit there, of course. [laughs] We could stand at the window and listen. But the rowak was like the para's newspaper. Now, look outside. That house opposite, the yellow one, was the Ghoshes'. Three brothers, joint family. It is a six-storey building now. The promoter came in 2016, 2017, gave them flats and money, and the brothers went to Salt Lake and one to Pune. No rowak. There is a gate and a guard.

I: How do you feel about those changes?

R: Mixed. I am not one of those people who say everything old was good. The old houses were falling down, honestly. Damp, no proper toilets in some. My own house, the roof leaks in two places, and the promoters come to me every six months. "Mashima, think about it, you will get two flats and fifty lakh." My son says sell it, come to Bangalore. But if I sell, where do I go? To a flat in New Town where I know nobody? At my age? [pause] What I feel sad about is that the new buildings are closed. People go in, close the door, go out in the car. I have lived opposite that building for eight years and I know two families in it. Two, out of eighteen.

I: Who lives in those new flats?

R: Mixed people. Some Bengali families from other parts of the city, young couples, both working. And many non-Bengali, people from outside, Marwari families, some from Bihar, one Punjabi family, and some young boys and girls renting, working in IT in Sector Five. Now I will be honest with you, because you are recording and I want to be honest. Some of the old residents do not like it. They say "outsiders are coming, the para's character is going." At the club meeting last year there was a big argument about whether to have the Puja announcements in Hindi also. One senior person said "this is a Bengali para, if they want to live here they should learn Bangla."

I: What did you think about that argument?

R: I thought it was foolish, frankly. [laughs] My father came from East Bengal in 1950, after Partition. With nothing, one trunk. The people here called him "Bangal" [a somewhat mocking term for people from East Bengal] for years. He was the outsider then! So I said this in the meeting. I said, "Forty years back you were calling my father an outsider, and today you are the ones protecting the para from outsiders?" Some people were angry with me. But the young boys in the club, they agreed. They have friends from everywhere. For them it is not a big thing.

I: That is a powerful example. Do the newer residents take part in the Puja now?

R: Slowly. The Agarwal family in the new building, their daughter dances in the cultural programme. Last year the Bihari tenants, three boys, they helped carry the idol for immersion, and after that the club boys started calling them for cricket. So it happens, but it happens through the young people, not through us old people. We are... we are a bit stuck. [pause] And also, you know, many of them are tenants. They stay two years, three years, and then they move. How much can you invest in a friendship with someone who is going to leave? That is how people think.

I: Let us talk a little about everyday life here. What is it like to live here in terms of services, water, roads, that kind of thing?

R: Water is fine here, I will give the Corporation that. We have had the tap water since my grandfather's time, it comes in the morning and evening. The problem is the monsoon. Every year, every single year, the lane goes under water. Jol jome [water collects, waterlogging], knee-deep, sometimes more. In 2021 the water came inside the ground floor. My father's books, which I had kept in the almirah downstairs, all spoiled. I cried that day, honestly.

I: What happens when the lane floods?

R: Everything stops. The rickshaw-wallahs charge three times. The maid cannot come. The old people cannot go to the doctor. And the drain water mixes with it, so there is a smell, and then dengue comes in September. We complain, the councillor sends the pump, but the pump comes after two days when the water has already gone down by itself. [laughs] There is a joke in the para that the pump is only for the photograph in the newspaper.

I: Have residents tried to do anything together about it?

R: Yes, there was a time, maybe five, six years back, when some of us made a committee and went to the borough office with a petition. Two hundred signatures. The officer was polite, he said the main drain on the big road is old, British-time, and needs replacement, and it is in the plan. It is still in the plan. [laughs] After that people lost interest. You know how it is, the energy goes. And there is also a feeling that if you are not with the party, your work will not happen. The club is close to the councillor, so if the club asks, sometimes things happen faster. That is the reality.

I: How do you feel about that, that access goes through the club?

R: It is uncomfortable. The club does good work, I told you, they helped me so much when my husband died. But the same club collects Puja donations, and if you give less, there are comments. And some families who do not get along with the club, they have more difficulty. For example, there is a Muslim family at the end of the lane, the tailor Rafiq-bhai. His family has been there longer than half the para, since his grandfather's time. But when there was the problem with his electricity meter, nobody from the club came forward. He had to run around alone. I felt ashamed about that. So the closeness I was talking about, it is real, but it is not for everybody equally.

I: Thank you for being so open about that. You mentioned earlier that as a girl you could not sit on the rowak. Can you tell me more about how it was for women in the para?

R: Oh, very different. My mother went out only with my father or with a group of women, to the market or to the temple. I was allowed to go to school and college, my father was progressive in that way, but I had to be home before dark. Sandhye [dusk], that was the rule. When the conch shell blew in the evening, all girls inside. [laughs] And I felt safe, you know, because everybody knew me. If some boy from another para passed a comment, the para boys would go and fight with him. It was a protection, but it was also a watching. Everybody was watching everybody.

I: And now?

R: Now my daughter-in-law, when she visits, she comes back at eleven at night from her friends' house and nobody says anything. That is a good change. But at the same time, I will tell you, I myself feel less safe now in the evening than when I was young. Isn't that strange? Earlier the lane was full of people till late, the rowak, the shops, children playing. Now after nine it is empty. The new buildings have their guards, but the lane itself is empty. The streetlight near the bend has not worked for months. My granddaughter is thirteen, when she comes in the summer holidays, I do not let her go to the corner shop alone after dark. And there was a chain snatching near the bus stop in December, a lady from the next para. Two boys on a motorbike.

I: So you feel the lane was safer when it was busier.

R: Exactly. Busy is safe. Empty is not safe. Nobody planned it that way, but that is how it was. Now everybody is inside with their TV and phone.

I: You said your son lives in Bangalore. Can you tell me about your children and how they relate to the para?

R: I have one son and one daughter. My daughter is in Chicago, married, two children. My son is in Bangalore, in software. Both left after college. Nothing here, they said, no jobs. That is the story of every house in this para. Go to any house, you will find an old couple or a widow like me, and the children in Bangalore, Pune, America. We joke that the para is an old-age home now. [pause] My son, when he comes for Puja, he goes to the club, he meets his old friends who are still here, he is very happy for those four days. But he says he cannot live here. "Ma, what will I do here? Where is the work?" And his children, my grandchildren, they speak Bangla with an accent now. [laughs] They call it "Kolkata" like it is a holiday place.

I: Do you think your children feel they belong here?

R: They belong to the memory of it. That is different. [long pause] I think belonging is something you have to keep doing, you know? Going to the shop, attending the Puja meeting, knowing whose father is in hospital. If you stop doing it, it becomes a memory. My son belongs to the para of 1998. That para does not exist.

I: That is a beautiful way to put it. What about you, what would you need to keep belonging here as you get older?

R: [laughs] Good question. Mainly I need the people. Honestly, I am worried. My knee is bad, sometimes I cannot go down the stairs for two, three days. Who will check on me? The maid, yes, and Mitra-babu's wife, we have an arrangement, if she doesn't see my window open by nine o'clock, she will call. That is my security system. [laughs] There was talk of starting a senior citizens' group in the club room, in the mornings, because there are so many of us alone now. I would like that. Somewhere to sit and have tea and talk. Like a rowak for old ladies! [laughs]

I: If you could change one thing about the neighbourhood, what would it be?

R: One thing? The drains. [laughs] No, seriously, the drains and the waterlogging. That is practical. But if you ask me what I really want... I want the new people and the old people to actually know each other. Not just nod. Know each other. Invite them for the bhog [the shared meal after Puja], properly, not as a formality. Make the Puja committee include tenants. If a boy from Bihar is living here three years, he is from this para. Why not? My father became from this para. Everybody was an outsider once.

I: Is there anything else you would like to add that we have not talked about?

R: Only that... please write in your report that old people are not just a problem. We are the ones holding the memory. When the promoter breaks a house, it is not just bricks. There was a jackfruit tree in the Ghosh house, all the children of the para climbed that tree. Now it is a parking space. I am not saying stop development. I am saying remember what was there. That is all.

I: Thank you so much, Shyamali-di. This has been really valuable.

R: Have one more cup of tea before you go. The biscuits are from the shop at the corner, the same shop since 1974. [laughs]

[End of recording]
